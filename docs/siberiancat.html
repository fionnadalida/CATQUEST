Siberian Cat

History & Origin

The Siberian is a naturally occurring cat breed from Russia and is traditionally associated with the forested and subarctic regions of Siberia. The breed has a long history, with references to Siberian cats dating back to around 1000 AD. These cats developed naturally in a demanding climate and were valued by Russian farmers and tradespeople for their ability to control rodents.

Siberian cats were known in Russia for centuries before becoming established as a formal pedigree breed. Early references to Siberian-type cats also appeared outside Russia. They were exhibited at cat shows during the late 19th century, including appearances in England and at Madison Square Garden in New York. Despite these early references, detailed breeding records were limited until the development of organized cat breeding in Russia during the 1980s.

In the late 1980s, Russian breeders began establishing formal standards for the Siberian. The Kotofei Cat Club in Moscow developed an early breed standard using two cats as important examples: Mars, a blue lynx point and white male, and Roman, a brown tabby and white male. Siberians were subsequently shown at organized Russian cat shows, helping establish the breed's modern pedigree.

Siberians began reaching North America in significant numbers during the 1990s. Elizabeth Terrell imported three Siberians to the United States in 1990, helping establish the breed there. TICA accepted the Siberian into its New Breed program in 1992 and granted championship status in 1996. CFA later accepted the breed for registration in 2000 and granted championship status in 2007. Today, the Siberian is recognized for its powerful build, weather-resistant triple coat, intelligence, athleticism, and affectionate nature.

Physical Characteristic

Important: Siberians are slow-maturing cats and may take as long as five years to reach full physical maturity. Their coat also changes noticeably between winter and summer.
Traits Details
Size Medium to large, powerful, and heavily built. Males are generally larger than females.
Weight Mature males commonly weigh around 12–18 pounds, while females commonly weigh around 8–12 pounds. Some individuals may be larger.
Body Strong, muscular, and substantial with a broad chest, heavy boning, rounded contours, and a characteristic barrel-shaped appearance.
Head Broad modified wedge with rounded contours, broad cheekbones, a rounded muzzle, and a gentle facial expression.
Eyes Large and nearly round, with colors including green, gold, green-gold, or copper. White cats may have blue or odd-colored eyes.
Ears Medium-sized, broad at the base, rounded at the tips, and well furnished with hair.
Legs Strong, muscular, and heavily boned, with the hind legs slightly longer than the front legs.
Feet Large and rounded with desirable tufts of fur between the toes.
Tail Medium in length, broad at the base, heavily furnished, and tapering slightly toward a blunt tip.
Neck Short to medium in length and substantial, often surrounded by a prominent ruff in mature cats.
Common Colors A wide variety of colors and patterns, including brown, black, red, cream, blue, silver, tabby, tortoiseshell, bicolor, and colorpoint varieties.
Distinctive Feature Powerful rounded body, large rounded feet, bushy tail, prominent neck ruff, and a dense weather-resistant triple coat that changes with the seasons.

Coat & Grooming

Coat Type: Medium to long semi-longhair coat consisting of three layers: guard hairs, awn hairs, and a dense undercoat. The coat is thick and weather-resistant.

Coat Pattern: Available in a very wide range of colors and patterns, including solid, tabby, tortoiseshell, bicolor, silver, shaded, and colorpoint patterns.

Grooming Frequency: Weekly combing or brushing is generally appropriate. More frequent grooming is needed during the heavy spring and fall shedding periods to remove the loose undercoat and prevent mats.

Bathing: Bathing is rarely necessary because the coat is naturally water-resistant. When bathing is required, the dense coat may take considerable time to become fully wet and dry.

Shedding: Moderate throughout the year with substantial seasonal shedding in spring and fall as the thick winter coat is replaced.

Unique Feature: The Siberian's triple coat changes according to the season, becoming especially thick and full during winter and shorter and less dense during warmer weather.

Longhair Variation: The Siberian is naturally a semi-longhaired breed and does not have a separate recognized longhair variety.

Characteristic & Temperament

Traits Details
Temperament Gentle, affectionate, playful, confident, adaptable, and generally easy-going.
Activity Level Moderate to high. Siberians are athletic jumpers and climbers and need regular opportunities for physical activity.
Intelligence Highly intelligent and curious. They are natural problem solvers and can learn tricks, leash walking, and interactive games.
Sociability Very social and family-oriented. They enjoy staying near their people but are generally not excessively demanding.
Affection Very affectionate and often enjoy cuddling and lap time while still maintaining an independent side.
Other Pets Generally good with children, other cats, and cat-friendly dogs when properly socialized and introduced.
Ideal Environment A home that provides companionship, climbing structures, interactive toys, scratching posts, and opportunities to explore and exercise.

Health & Care Consideration

Common Health Concerns

Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM) - A condition in which the heart muscle becomes abnormally thick. Siberians should be monitored for signs of heart disease, particularly when there is a known family history of HCM.

Polycystic Kidney Disease (PKD) - A hereditary kidney disorder involving cyst formation in the kidneys. Responsible breeding programs can use genetic and veterinary screening to reduce hereditary disease risks.

Obesity - Siberians have a naturally large, substantial body, but excess fat can increase the risk of diabetes, arthritis, and other health problems. Measured feeding and regular activity are important.

Hip Dysplasia - A developmental abnormality of the hip joint that can cause pain, stiffness, or difficulty jumping. Maintaining a healthy weight can help reduce stress on the joints.

Injuries - Siberians are powerful climbers and jumpers and may attempt to reach high or difficult locations. Secure shelves, furniture, and other climbing areas to reduce the risk of falls and injuries.

Dental Disease - Like other cats, Siberians can develop plaque, tartar, and periodontal disease. Regular dental care and veterinary examinations help maintain oral health.

Care Considerations

Nutrition: Provide a complete and balanced diet appropriate for the cat's age, size, activity level, and health condition. Measured meals are useful because Siberians can be prone to excessive weight gain.

Weight Management: Monitor body condition regularly rather than judging weight from appearance alone, since the thick coat can make a Siberian appear heavier than it is. Avoid excessive treats and adjust portions when necessary.

Exercise & Enrichment: Provide daily interactive play, climbing structures, sturdy cat trees, puzzle feeders, wand toys, and opportunities for exploration. Siberians can also enjoy harness training and learning tricks.

Dental Care: Brush the teeth regularly with cat-safe dental products and arrange routine veterinary dental examinations to reduce plaque and periodontal disease.

Nail Care: Trim the claws regularly, approximately every few weeks or as needed, and provide sturdy scratching posts or towers.

Eye Care: Special eye care is generally not required. Check the eyes periodically for redness, discharge, cloudiness, or other changes and seek veterinary attention if abnormalities appear.

Ear Care: No special ear care is generally required. Check periodically for redness, excessive wax, odor, or discharge and consult a veterinarian if abnormalities appear.

Veterinary Care: Schedule regular veterinary examinations to monitor heart health, weight, joints, dental condition, and general wellbeing. Keep vaccinations and parasite prevention up to date.

Genetic Testing: Responsible breeders should use appropriate genetic and health screening, particularly for inherited conditions such as HCM and PKD. Genetic diversity and careful selection of breeding pairs are important for maintaining the breed's health.

Important: Breed-associated health conditions are potential risks and do not mean that every Siberian will develop them.
