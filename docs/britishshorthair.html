British Shorthair Cat

History & Origin

The British Shorthair traces its ancestry to the domestic shorthair cats that lived throughout Britain. These cats are generally believed to have descended from European domestic cats brought to Britain by the Romans during their invasion in the first century AD. They became established as working cats in homes, farms, and other settlements, where their hunting ability made them useful for controlling rodents.

For centuries, these British domestic cats were primarily valued as working animals rather than as a formally established pedigree breed. They developed a robust build, dense short coat, and strong hunting ability suited to Britain's climate. Their calm and confident nature also eventually made them valued as household companions.

The development of the modern British Shorthair began during the rise of the cat fancy in the late 19th century. Harrison Weir, an English artist and naturalist known as the "Father of the Cat Fancy," admired the British domestic shorthair and began selectively breeding these cats to establish a consistent type. He also helped establish organized cat shows and developed early judging standards.

The first organized cat show in Britain was held at Crystal Palace in London on July 13, 1871. Among the 170 exhibits were the cats then described as "English Shorthairs," the forerunners of today's British Shorthair. A 14-year-old blue tabby female owned by Harrison Weir received Best in Show.

During the late 19th and early 20th centuries, British Shorthairs became prominent in cat shows. The breed was originally particularly associated with the blue coat, and cats of this type were sometimes referred to as "British Blues." However, British Shorthairs were already being exhibited in a variety of colors and patterns.

The breed was severely affected by World War I. After the war, the number of suitable registered British Shorthairs had decreased, so breeders used unregistered domestic shorthairs to maintain the breeding population. These crosses sometimes produced cats that lacked the desired breed type. Breeders subsequently used Persians to help restore the British Shorthair's desired structure and coat characteristics.

The breed was again affected by World War II, which further reduced its breeding population. After the war, breeders used several other breeds in carefully managed outcrossing programs, including Persians, Russian Blues, Burmese, and Chartreux, to maintain genetic diversity and restore the desired British type. The use of Persians also introduced the longhair gene into the British breeding population.

Longhaired kittens continued to appear in British Shorthair litters. For many years they were generally treated as variants or placed as pets. Eventually, breeders developed the longhaired cats as a separate breed. The British Longhair was officially recognized by the GCCF in 2017 and received GCCF Championship status in 2021.

British Shorthairs were imported to the United States in the early 1900s. They were initially registered there as domestic shorthairs, while formal recognition developed later. The Cat Fanciers' Association granted the breed championship status in May 1980.

Physical Characteristic

Important: British Shorthairs mature slowly and may take approximately 3 to 5 years to reach full physical development.
Traits Details
Size Medium to large
Weight Females: 7 to 12 lb (3.2 to 5.4 kg); males: 9 to 17 lb (4.1 to 7.7 kg).
Body Compact, powerful, muscular, and well-balanced with a broad chest
Head Large, round, and broad with full cheeks
Eyes Large, round, and well-opened
Ears Medium-sized, broad at the base, and set well apart
Legs Short to medium length, strong, and well-boned
Feet Rounded and firm
Tail Thick at the base, medium in length, and rounded at the tip
Neck Long, muscular, and proportional to the body
Common Colors Blue, white, black, lilac, chocolate, red, cream, fawn, cinnamon, and others
Distinctive Feature Round head, full cheeks, prominent whisker pads, and a broad, compact body

Coat & Grooming

Coat Type: Short, extremely dense, plush, and resilient.

Coat Pattern: Available in numerous patterns, including solid, tabby, tortoiseshell, bi-colour, tipped, and colourpointed.

Grooming Frequency: Weekly brushing or combing; more frequent grooming during seasonal shedding (both applicable to Shorthair and Longhair).

Bathing: Generally unnecessary unless the coat becomes soiled; bathing may be used for show preparation.

Shedding: Moderate; shedding increases during seasonal coat changes.

Unique Feature: Exceptionally dense, plush coat with a crisp texture.

Longhair Variation: British Longhair; developed from the longhair gene introduced through historical outcrossing, particularly with Persians.

Characteristic & Temperament

Traits Details
Temperament Calm, gentle, patient, and easygoing
Activity Level Low to moderate; enjoys play but is generally not highly active
Intelligence Intelligent and observant; learns household routines
Sociability Affectionate and forms strong bonds with family members
Affection Enjoys staying near people but generally prefers sitting beside them rather than being carried or held
Other Pets Generally tolerant of dogs and other pets when properly introduced
Ideal Environment Adaptable to houses and apartments; benefits from regular play and opportunities for activity

Health & Care Consideration

Common Health Concerns

Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM) - A heart condition involving abnormal thickening of the heart muscle. HCM can occur in British Shorthairs, and responsible breeders may screen breeding cats through veterinary cardiac examinations.

Polycystic Kidney Disease (PKD) - An inherited condition involving the development of cysts in the kidneys. GCCF notes that PKD was historically a concern in the breed and is now controlled through the use of available DNA testing by responsible breeders.

Obesity - British Shorthairs can be prone to excessive weight gain because of their relatively sedentary lifestyle and food intake.

Dental Disease - British Shorthairs, like other cats, can develop dental disease. Regular dental care and veterinary examinations are recommended.

Care Considerations

Nutrion: Provide a complete and balanced diet appropriate for the cat's age, size, and activity level.

Weight Management: British Shorthairs have a relatively low activity level and can gain excess weight. Food portions should be monitored and regular activity encouraged.

Exercise & Enrichment: Provide regular play and activities such as interactive toys and food puzzles to encourage movement and mental stimulation.

Dental Care: Brush the teeth regularly using veterinarian-approved cat toothpaste and arrange professional dental care when recommended.

Nail Care: Trim the nails regularly to prevent overgrowth.

Eye Care: Check the eyes regularly for discharge, redness, cloudiness, or irritation. Keep the area around the eyes clean and consult a veterinarian if unusual changes or persistent discharge occur.

Ear Care: Check and clean the ears regularly to remove excess wax and debris. Use a gentle, veterinarian-recommended ear cleaner and seek veterinary attention if there is redness, foul odor, or unusual discharge.

Veterinary Care: Regular veterinary examinations are recommended for preventive care and monitoring.

Genetic Testing: Ask breeders about available PKD testing and HCM screening of breeding cats.

Important: Breed-associated health conditions are potential risks and do not mean that every British Shorthair will develop them.
