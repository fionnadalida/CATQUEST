Burmese Cat

History & Origin

The Burmese cat originated from cats native to Southeast Asia, particularly areas corresponding to present-day Myanmar (Burma), Thailand, and the Malay Peninsula. Cats resembling the Burmese were known in the region for centuries, but the modern Burmese breed was developed in the United States during the 20th century. Earlier Burmese-type cats were also brought to England in the late 19th century, where they were sometimes mistaken for chocolate-colored Siamese cats. Due to the popularity of blue-eyed, seal-point Siamese cats, these Burmese-type cats received less attention and eventually disappeared from the British cat scene.

The development of the modern Burmese breed began in 1930, when a sailor brought a small, walnut-brown female cat named Wong Mau from Burma to San Francisco, California. She was given to Dr. Joseph G. Thompson, who noticed that she had a distinct appearance compared to the Siamese cats of that time. Wong Mau had a compact, muscular body, a short tail, a rounded head, a broad muzzle, and darker brown coloring on her face, legs, and tail. Although many breeders initially considered her to be a dark Siamese, Dr. Thompson believed that her characteristics were unique and worth investigating.

To establish a new breed, Dr. Thompson worked with experienced breeders and researchers, including Virginia Cobb, Billie Gerst, and Dr. Clyde E. Keeler. Since there were very few cats with Wong Mau's appearance available for breeding, she was paired with a Seal Point Siamese named Tai Mau, who had been imported from Thailand. Their kittens displayed different physical characteristics: some resembled Siamese cats, while others had brown coats similar to Wong Mau, with darker points. These results showed that the kittens carried different combinations of coat-color traits.

Wong Mau was later bred with her son, Yen Yen Mau, resulting in kittens with three distinct appearances: Siamese-like kittens, brown kittens resembling Wong Mau, and darker, solid brown kittens. The darker brown offspring became the foundation of the modern Burmese breed. Further breeding experiments helped establish the distinctive coat color and physical characteristics that breeders wanted to preserve. Later genetic understanding revealed that Wong Mau was a Siamese–Burmese hybrid, now considered an early Tonkinese-type cat.

In 1934, the Burmese was proposed to the Cat Fanciers' Association (CFA) as a distinct breed, and it received approval for stud-book registration in 1936. During the breed's early development, breeders continued crossing Burmese cats with Siamese cats to increase the small breeding population. However, this resulted in many hybrid offspring, making it difficult to maintain consistent Burmese characteristics. In 1947, CFA suspended Burmese registration and required breeders to establish three generations of pure Burmese ancestry before cats could be registered again. Breeders gradually developed sufficient pure Burmese lineages, allowing registration to resume in 1957.

The breed also began expanding beyond the United States during the late 1940s. In 1949, Burmese cats were imported to England, helping establish the breed in the United Kingdom. The Burmese was officially recognized by the Governing Council of the Cat Fancy (GCCF) in 1952, marking an important step in its development in Europe. In 1955, the first recorded blue Burmese kitten, named Sealcoat Blue Surprise, was born in England. Over time, additional coat colors, including chocolate, blue, and lilac, became established through selective breeding. Red and cream variations, along with tortoiseshell patterns, were later introduced into European Burmese breeding programs.

In 1958, the United Burmese Cat Fanciers worked toward establishing a consistent breed standard, which was adopted by CFA in 1959. This standard emphasized the Burmese's rounded head, short muzzle, round eyes, compact body, and muscular build. As breeding programs developed in different regions, the American and European Burmese gradually developed differences in their physical appearance and breed standards.

Physical Characteristic

Important: Burmese cats are often described as having a “small bulldog” appearance because of their compact, muscular build and substantial weight relative to their size. They are considered fully grown at around 18 months according to CFA.
Traits Details
Size Medium-sized, compact
Weight Females: 6 to 9 lb (2.7 to 4.1 kg); males: 11 to 14 lb (5.0 to 6.4 kg).
Body Compact, muscular, substantial bone structure; surprisingly heavy for its size
Head Rounded, broad, with a short and broad muzzle
Eyes Large, rounded, widely spaced; usually yellow to gold
Ears Medium-sized, broad at the base, rounded at the tips, set well apart
Legs Well-proportioned and relatively slender
Feet Round, with five toes in front and four behind
Tail Medium in length, straight, moderately thick, tapering toward the tip
Neck Well-developed and supports the rounded head
Common Colors Sable/brown, champagne/chocolate, blue, platinum/lilac; GCCF also recognizes red, cream, and tortoiseshell variations
Distinctive Feature Compact muscular body, rounded head, large golden eyes, short broad muzzle, and surprisingly heavy build

Coat & Grooming

Coat Type: Short, fine, smooth, close-lying.

Coat Pattern: Solid color with subtle darker shading toward the head, ears, legs, and tail.

Grooming Frequency: Once a week.

Bathing: Not regularly required; occasional bathing may be done when necessary.

Shedding: Minimal

Unique Feature: Satin-like, glossy coat that lies closely against the body

Longhair Variation: No standard longhair variation

Characteristic & Temperament

Traits Details
Temperament Affectionate, confident, friendly
Activity Level Playful, energetic, athletic
Intelligence Highly intelligent and curious
Sociability Very people-oriented and social
Affection Enjoys laps, physical contact, and following people around
Other Pets Usually adaptable to cats and dogs when properly introduced
Ideal Environment Houses or apartments where the cat receives regular attention, play and companionship

Health & Care Consideration

Common Health Concerns

Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM) - A heart condition involving abnormal thickening of the heart muscle. Burmese cats can be affected by HCM, and screening through appropriate veterinary cardiac examinations can help identify heart abnormalities.

Diabetes Mellitus - Burmese cats may have an increased risk of developing diabetes. Maintaining a healthy body condition, monitoring food intake and having regular veterinary examinations can help with early detection and management.

Feline Orofacial Pain Syndrome (FOPS) - A condition involving episodes of pain around the face and mouth that has been reported more frequently in Burmese cats. Episodes may be associated with eating, grooming or opening the mouth. Veterinary assessment is recommended if signs of oral or facial pain appear.

Hypokalemic Polymyopathy - A genetic condition that can cause episodes of muscle weakness. Affected kittens may have difficulty holding their heads upright or walking. Genetic testing can help identify carriers and reduce the risk of producing affected kittens.

Dental Disease - Burmese cats, like other cats, can develop dental problems. Regular tooth brushing and veterinary dental examinations can help maintain oral health.

Care Considerations

Nutrion: Provide a complete and balanced diet appropriate for the cat's age, size, lifestyle and health requirements. Fresh, clean water should always be available.

Weight Management: Monitor food portions and body condition regularly. Burmese cats have a naturally solid and muscular build, which can sometimes make excess weight difficult to recognize. Maintaining an appropriate body condition is particularly important because obesity is associated with diabetes in cats.

Exercise & Enrichment: Provide daily play, interactive toys, puzzle feeders, climbing opportunities and other activities that encourage movement and mental stimulation. Burmese cats are intelligent and active and benefit from regular interaction.

Dental Care: Brush the teeth regularly using veterinarian-approved cat toothpaste and arrange professional dental care when recommended.

Nail Care: Trim the nails regularly to prevent overgrowth and provide an appropriate scratching post or scratching surface.

Eye Care: The eyes generally do not require special treatment, but they can be gently checked and cleaned when normal tearing or buildup occurs. Unusual discharge, redness or irritation should be examined by a veterinarian.

Ear Care: Check the ears regularly for redness, unusual odor, discharge or debris. Changes in the ears may indicate an underlying problem requiring veterinary attention.

Veterinary Care: Regular veterinary examinations are recommended for preventive care, vaccinations, dental health, weight monitoring and early detection of disease. Cardiac screening can also be discussed with a veterinarian because of the breed's susceptibility to HCM.

Genetic Testing: When obtaining a Burmese kitten from a breeder, ask about available genetic testing and the health history of the parents, particularly for conditions such as hypokalemic polymyopathy.

Important: Breed-associated health conditions are potential risks and do not mean that every Burmese will develop them.
