Scottish Fold Cat

History & Origin

The Scottish Fold originated from a spontaneous genetic mutation found among farm cats in Scotland. In 1961, shepherd William Ross discovered a white female cat named Susie on a farm in the Tayside region of Scotland. Susie had unusual ears that folded forward and downward, giving her a distinctive appearance.

William Ross became interested in Susie's unusual ears and obtained one of her kittens, a female named Snooks. Snooks became an important foundation cat for the breed. She was bred with domestic cats and British Shorthairs, producing kittens that inherited the unusual folded-ear characteristic. The breeding program gradually established the rounded body, head, eyes, and feet that became characteristic of the Scottish Fold.

The breed was initially known as the Lop-eared cat. Scottish Fold cats were later introduced to the United States, where breeders continued developing the breed using controlled outcrosses. American Shorthairs and British Shorthairs became important outcross breeds. Every Scottish Fold can trace its ancestry back to the original Scottish cats associated with Susie.

Scottish Folds were recognized by major cat organizations as the breed became established. The shorthaired variety received CFA championship status in 1978, while the longhaired variety achieved championship status in 1992. Straight-eared offspring are also part of the Scottish breeding program and are recognized in separate divisions by some associations. Scottish Folds are now known for their folded ears, large round eyes, rounded bodies, and affectionate personalities.

Physical Characteristic

Important: The same genetic mutation responsible for the folded ears is associated with abnormal cartilage and bone development. The tail should always be handled gently, and stiffness, reduced mobility, or signs of pain require veterinary attention.
Traits Details
Size Medium-sized, generally 7–10 pounds, with a compact and sturdy appearance.
Weight Generally around 7–10 pounds, although individual cats may vary in size.
Body Rounded, sturdy, and well-padded body with medium boning and a solid feel.
Head Large and distinctly rounded, with prominent cheeks, a rounded muzzle, firm chin, and short neck.
Eyes Large, round, and expressive, giving the breed its characteristic sweet and open expression.
Ears Small to medium-sized ears that fold forward and downward toward the skull. Kittens are born with straight ears, with folding developing at around three to four weeks in cats carrying the fold gene.
Legs Medium in length, sturdy, and proportionate to the rounded body.
Feet Rounded feet with a neat appearance, contributing to the breed's overall rounded form.
Tail Medium to long and flexible, with particular importance placed on maintaining a healthy, freely moving tail.
Neck Short and substantial, blending smoothly into the rounded head and body.
Common Colors Available in a wide range of solid colors, tabbies, tortoiseshells, bicolors, pointed patterns, and other recognized color and pattern combinations.
Distinctive Feature Forward-folding ears that create an owl-like appearance, combined with large round eyes and an overall rounded body structure.

Coat & Grooming

Coat Type: The shorthair has a dense, plush coat. The longhair variety has a semi-long, softer coat that stands away from the body.

Coat Pattern: Scottish Folds can occur in many colors and patterns, including solid, tabby, tortoiseshell, bicolor, and pointed patterns.

Grooming Frequency: Shorthair Scottish Folds generally require weekly brushing. Longhair Scottish Folds benefit from regular weekly combing to remove loose hair and reduce tangles.

Bathing: Occasional bathing is generally sufficient when necessary. The appropriate products and bathing routine should be selected according to the individual cat's coat and skin condition.

Shedding: Light to moderate throughout the year, with heavier seasonal shedding possible during spring and fall.

Unique Feature: The coat is plush and dense in the shorthair variety, while the longhair variety has a softer semi-long coat that gives the breed an even fuller appearance.

Longhair Variation: The Scottish Fold is available in both shorthair and longhair varieties. The longhair variety has the same general body and head characteristics but carries a semi-long coat.

Characteristic & Temperament

Traits Details
Temperament Sweet, calm, affectionate, intelligent, curious, and generally laid-back.
Activity Level Moderate. Scottish Folds are less energetic than some active breeds but still need regular interactive play and movement.
Intelligence Intelligent and inquisitive. They can learn tricks, solve simple puzzles, play fetch, and investigate their surroundings.
Sociability Social and strongly attached to their families. Many enjoy following their people around the home rather than remaining isolated.
Affection Affectionate and people-oriented. Many prefer staying close to their owners while keeping all four feet on a stable surface rather than being carried constantly.
Other Pets Generally compatible with children, other cats, and cat-friendly dogs when introductions are gradual and properly managed.
Ideal Environment A calm indoor home with regular human companionship, interactive play, comfortable resting areas, scratching posts, and opportunities for gentle activity.

Health & Care Consideration

Common Health Concerns

Osteochondrodysplasia - A hereditary cartilage and bone disorder associated with the same mutation that produces the folded ears. It can cause abnormal bone development, stiff joints, reduced mobility, and pain. Severity varies between individual cats.

Degenerative Joint Disease - Scottish Folds can develop progressive joint problems, particularly affecting the tail, ankles, feet, and other joints. A stiff or painful tail and changes in movement should be evaluated by a veterinarian.

Hypertrophic Cardiomyopathy (HCM) - A heart condition in which the heart muscle becomes abnormally thick. Veterinary monitoring is important because affected cats may not show obvious signs during the early stages.

Polycystic Kidney Disease (PKD) - A hereditary kidney disorder that can occur in cats with Persian ancestry and related breeding lines. Responsible breeders can use genetic screening to reduce the risk of passing known mutations to kittens.

Obesity - Scottish Folds can gain excess weight because of their relatively relaxed activity level. Controlled portions and regular interactive exercise are important for maintaining a healthy body condition.

Care Considerations

Nutrition: Provide a complete and balanced diet appropriate for the cat's age, size, activity level, and health condition. Measured portions help prevent excessive weight gain.

Weight Management: Monitor body condition regularly and avoid excessive treats. Maintaining a healthy weight is particularly important because additional weight can place extra stress on already vulnerable joints.

Exercise & Enrichment: Provide gentle daily play using interactive toys, puzzle feeders, climbing areas, and activities that encourage movement without placing excessive strain on the joints.

Dental Care: Regular tooth brushing with cat-safe dental products can help reduce plaque and tartar. Dental health should also be evaluated during routine veterinary examinations.

Nail Care: Trim the claws regularly, approximately every few weeks or as needed, and provide suitable scratching surfaces.

Eye Care: Check the large eyes periodically for redness, excessive tearing, discharge, cloudiness, or other changes. Veterinary attention should be sought when abnormalities appear.

Ear Care: Check the folded ears regularly for excessive wax, discharge, redness, odor, or irritation. Clean only when necessary and avoid inserting objects deeply into the ear canal.

Veterinary Care: Regular veterinary examinations are especially important for monitoring joint health, mobility, heart health, kidney health, dental condition, and body weight. Any stiffness, abnormal gait, reluctance to jump, or signs of pain should be assessed promptly.

Genetic Testing: Responsible breeding requires careful genetic planning. Fold-eared cats should not be bred together because inheriting two copies of the fold-associated mutation can cause severe skeletal abnormalities. Genetic testing and appropriate outcrossing help breeders reduce health risks.

Important: Breed-associated health conditions are potential risks and do not mean that every Scottish Fold will develop them. The folded-ear mutation is directly associated with cartilage and bone abnormalities, so responsible breeding and careful veterinary monitoring are especially important.
